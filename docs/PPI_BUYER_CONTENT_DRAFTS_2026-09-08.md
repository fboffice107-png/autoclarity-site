## Draft 1 — Used-car buyer checklist

- **Working title:** Used-Car Buyer Checklist: What to Confirm Before You Commit
- **Primary intent:** A buyer wants a practical checklist before agreeing to buy
  a used vehicle or arranging an independent inspection.
- **Suggested URL:** `/guides/used-car-buyer-checklist/`
- **H1:** Used-Car Buyer Checklist: What to Confirm Before You Commit
- **Meta description:** Use this practical checklist to verify the vehicle,
  seller access, records, inspection conditions, and next steps before buying a
  used car.
- **30-script connections:** Days 6–10, 14, and 16. Use the existing scripts and
  permitted real footage; do not add publishing volume.

### Page body

Buying a used car gets easier when you separate three questions:

1. Is this the vehicle the listing describes?
2. What can you verify before money changes hands?
3. What still needs a closer professional or specialist review?

A clean listing, a short drive, or a seller's inspection can be useful, but none
answers every question. Use this checklist to organize the decision while you
still have time to ask for records, request access, and pause if important facts
are missing.

### Before you travel or schedule an inspection

- Confirm the year, make, model, trim, asking price, and current location.
- Ask for the VIN when it is available and compare it with the vehicle and
  paperwork before purchase. AutoClarity lets you start a request without a VIN,
  but it should be shared before the inspection when available so the request
  can be matched to the vehicle presented.
- Ask whether the seller will allow an independent mobile pre-purchase
  inspection. The seller controls access to the vehicle.
- Confirm who will be present with the keys and authority to provide access.
- Ask whether the inspector may access the interior, engine bay, trunk or cargo
  area, and available maintenance records.
- Ask whether a road test is permitted. A road test can occur only when the
  seller permits it and conditions are safe and lawful; it is never guaranteed.
- Confirm the location has safe, lawful working space. Low clearance, uneven
  ground, weather, property rules, or seller restrictions can limit underbody
  access and other checks.
- Request maintenance and repair records, recall information, and any available
  history report. Treat records and databases as separate evidence, not a
  replacement for examining the vehicle's present condition.

### During your own first look

You do not need to diagnose the car. Your goal is to record questions for a
closer review.

- Does the VIN on the vehicle match the listing or paperwork you were given?
- Are any dashboard warning lights illuminated after startup?
- Do all four tires appear to match, and can you see unusual wear, cracking, or
  sidewall damage?
- Are there visible leaks, fresh stains, strong odors, damaged glass or lamps,
  uneven body gaps, or differences in paint color or texture?
- Do the climate controls, windows, locks, exterior lighting, and other basic
  controls operate during the time you have with the vehicle?
- Does the seller's explanation match the records and the condition you can
  observe?

One clue rarely proves a cause. A stain does not establish where a leak began;
a warning light does not name the failed part; and even paint differences need
context. Photograph or write down the question instead of turning it into a
diagnosis.

### Las Vegas conditions worth discussing

For a Las Vegas-area vehicle, make sure the review does not stop at whether the
engine starts and the air feels cold for a few seconds. Cooling-system condition,
battery and charging behavior, air-conditioning operation, tire condition and
age, and heat or sun damage are sensible discussion points. They remain parts of
a whole-vehicle review, not a special test that can guarantee future performance.

### What to ask an independent inspector

Before booking, ask what is included and what depends on access. AutoClarity's
published scope covers vehicle identity comparison; observable body, glass,
lamp, interior, electronics, engine, fluid, visible leak, cooling, transmission,
battery, charging, steering, suspension, brake, tire, and underbody condition;
warning-light review; and a road test when authorized and safe. The exact scope
still depends on the vehicle, location, seller permission, safety, and approved
quote.

Also ask what you will receive in writing. AutoClarity's current report format
organizes meaningful photographs, immediate safety concerns, repair priorities,
estimated repair-cost ranges where appropriate, an overall condition score, and
a Proceed, Negotiate/Repair First, or Do Not Proceed recommendation. That
recommendation is decision support, not a warranty or a substitute for the
buyer's judgment.

### Before you commit money

- Read the full report, including limitations and items that could not be
  checked.
- Separate immediate safety concerns from maintenance, cosmetic, and uncertain
  items.
- Ask for specialist diagnosis when a finding, access limit, or vehicle type
  calls for it.
- Confirm title, ownership, lien, registration, financing, insurance, and sale
  terms through the appropriate records and professionals. A physical PPI does
  not perform those functions.
- Decide what evidence would change your offer, require repair first, or make
  you stop. Do not let a single score replace the underlying findings.

### CTA

Looking at a vehicle in the Las Vegas area? [Submit a free inspection
request](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/#request) with the vehicle and
location details. AutoClarity reviews the vehicle, location, scope, and seller
access before sending an exact quote and available times. A request does not
charge you or confirm an appointment.

### Claims and evidence notes

- Ground scope, VIN, road-test, underbody, request, report, and pricing language
  in `scripts/public-facts.json` and the current PPI page at publication time.
- The Las Vegas heat paragraph is a buyer prompt, not a claim that a particular
  measurement, diagnostic procedure, or outcome is included in every PPI.
- Add an original, permission-safe downloadable checklist or annotated demo

  seller identity, or report detail without documented permission.
- Do not add claims about savings, same-day availability, warranties, customers,
  credentials, or inspection success rates.

## Draft 2 — Independent PPI vs. dealer inspection

- **Working title:** Pre-Purchase Inspection vs. Dealer Inspection: What
  Used-Car Buyers Should Compare
- **Primary intent:** A buyer has been told that a vehicle was inspected and
  wants to know whether a separate buyer-selected PPI would answer different
  questions.
- **Suggested URL:** `/guides/pre-purchase-inspection-vs-dealer-inspection/`
- **H1:** Pre-Purchase Inspection vs. Dealer Inspection: What Should a Buyer
  Compare?
- **Meta description:** Learn how a buyer-selected pre-purchase inspection
  differs from a dealer or seller inspection—and which questions to ask before
  relying on either.
- **30-script connections:** Days 3, 5, 16, 19, 25, and 27. Reuse those talking
  points; do not create a separate social sequence.

### Page body

“It has already been inspected” is the beginning of a useful conversation, not
the end of one. A dealer inspection may provide valuable information. So may a
manufacturer-backed certification process, maintenance record, history report,
or government-required test. The important question is not which label sounds
best. It is what was actually checked, for whom, under what conditions, and what
the buyer can review.

### The practical differences

| Question | Dealer or seller-provided inspection | Buyer-selected independent PPI |
|---|---|---|
| Who arranged it? | The seller or dealer | The prospective buyer |
| Who defined the scope? | Varies by seller, dealer, program, or facility | The buyer selects the provider and reviews the quoted scope |
| What does “passed” mean? | It depends on the written checklist or program standards | The report should describe observations, priorities, limitations, and the inspector's recommendation framework |
| Who receives the findings? | Ask for the complete written result and date | The buyer receives the findings; AutoClarity reports to the buyer |
| Are repairs also being sold? | Sometimes, depending on the provider | AutoClarity does not sell the repairs identified by its PPI |
| Is it a warranty? | Only a separate written warranty can define warranty coverage | No; a PPI is a professional opinion about observable condition, not a warranty or guarantee |

These are categories, not judgments about every dealer or inspection program.
Some dealers provide detailed records and welcome outside inspectors. Some
seller-provided inspections are recent and thorough. A buyer-selected PPI still
has a distinct role: it lets the buyer choose the inspector, understand the
scope, and receive a report framed around the purchase decision.

### Ask to see the inspection, not just the claim

Request the dated checklist or report and ask:

- Does it identify the exact vehicle?
- Which systems and components were checked?
- Was the vehicle road-tested, and under what conditions?
- Was any underbody view performed, and was a lift used?
- Were measurements or photographs included?
- Were warning lights or diagnostic information reviewed?
- What was inaccessible, omitted, or outside scope?
- Were repairs performed afterward, and are the related records available?
- Does any separate warranty apply, and where are its written terms?

If the answers are clear and documented, they become useful inputs. If the only
answer is “our technician looked at it,” the buyer still does not know enough to
compare that review with an independent PPI.

### Certification, history, and inspection answer different questions

A certification program can describe a seller's standards and any included
benefits. A vehicle-history product can report information available in its
sources. A government VIN or emissions process serves its own regulatory
purpose. A physical PPI focuses on observable vehicle condition at a particular
time and within the access provided. None of these automatically replaces all
the others, and none can promise a defect-free vehicle.

### What if the dealer will not allow an outside inspection?

Start with neutral questions. The location may have insurance, scheduling,
security, lift, or test-drive rules. Ask whether a mobile inspector may work on
site, whether a dealership employee must remain present, what access is allowed,
and whether another safe location or approved facility is possible.

Do not assume refusal proves that the vehicle has a problem. It does change the
evidence available to you. Decide before placing nonrefundable money whether you
are comfortable proceeding without the inspection access you consider
important. AutoClarity cannot enter, drive, lift, scan, or otherwise inspect a
vehicle without permission and safe, lawful conditions.

### How AutoClarity frames the buyer's decision

AutoClarity's service is buyer-focused and does not include repair sales. The
report separates immediate safety concerns, higher-priority findings, maintenance
or moderate findings, measurements and observations, estimated repair-cost
ranges where appropriate, and inspection limitations. It concludes with a
Proceed, Negotiate/Repair First, or Do Not Proceed recommendation.

That recommendation does not guarantee the car or tell the seller what to do.
It gives the buyer a documented way to weigh the observed condition, uncertainty,
price, and next steps.

### CTA

If a Las Vegas-area seller or dealer permits an independent inspection, [submit
the vehicle and location for review](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/#request).
Submitting is free. AutoClarity confirms the exact scope, quote, access needs,
and available timing before an appointment is booked.

### Claims and evidence notes

- Preserve the comparison's neutral framing. Do not imply that every dealer
  inspection is conflicted, incomplete, or inferior.
- Verify AutoClarity's no-repair-sales, buyer-reporting, scope, and request-flow
  statements against the current PPI page and public facts before publication.
- Any discussion of a named certification, warranty, history provider, or
  government program requires current primary-source review and precise wording;
  none is needed for this draft to help the buyer.
- A founder video can demonstrate how to compare two fictional checklists. Do
  not display a real dealer document or customer report without permission.

## Draft 3 — What a PPI can and cannot catch

- **Working title:** What Can a Pre-Purchase Inspection Catch—and What Can It
  Miss?
- **Primary intent:** A buyer wants realistic expectations about physical
  checks, scan-tool information, access limits, and hidden or intermittent
  problems.
- **Suggested URL:**
  `/guides/what-a-pre-purchase-inspection-can-and-cannot-find/`
- **H1:** What a Pre-Purchase Inspection Can—and Cannot—Tell You
- **Meta description:** See what a used-car PPI can document, why access matters,
  what a scan can reveal, and which hidden or future problems no inspection can
  guarantee against.
- **30-script connections:** Days 4 and 11–14. Use real demonstration footage
  and the existing scripts rather than producing additional posts.

### Page body

A good pre-purchase inspection replaces a vague “looks fine” with documented
observations. It cannot turn a used vehicle into a known, risk-free object. The
most useful report states both sides clearly: what the inspector observed and
what the conditions did not allow the inspector to determine.

### What an inspection can document

Within the confirmed scope and available access, a PPI can document items such
as:

- vehicle identity and a VIN comparison;
- exterior body condition, glass, lamps, and observable signs that may justify
  questions about prior paint or repair;
- interior condition, electronics, instrument-cluster warnings, heating, and
  air-conditioning operation;
- engine operation, visible fluid condition and levels, visible leaks, cooling
  system observations, transmission operation, and battery or charging-system
  observations;
- steering, suspension, visible brakes, tire tread, approximate tire age, and
  observable wear or damage;
- accessible underbody areas where the location, permission, clearance, ground,
  safety, and equipment allow;
- road-test behavior when the seller authorizes the drive and conditions are
  safe and lawful; and
- meaningful findings, photographs, priorities, measurements, and next-step
  recommendations in a written report.

The wording matters. “No leak observed during this inspection” is different
from “this vehicle cannot leak.” “No warning light present” is different from
“every module and system is fault-free.” Observation should not be rewritten as
certainty.

### What a diagnostic scanner can tell you

When diagnostic scanning is expressly included in the approved scope, the
vehicle permits access, and the available tool supports the relevant systems, a
scanner may retrieve information such as stored or pending trouble codes and
supported data from communicating modules. That information can point to a
system that deserves closer investigation.

A code is not a complete diagnosis. It does not automatically identify the
failed part, prove the cause, set an exact repair cost, or predict when a failure
will occur. Tool coverage varies by vehicle and system. Modules may not
communicate, access may be refused, information may have been cleared, and an
intermittent problem may not be present during the inspection. The absence of a
code is not a guarantee that the car has no mechanical, electrical, body, tire,
brake, maintenance, or future problem.

Because AutoClarity's currently published core scope promises warning-light
review rather than universal scan-tool testing, buyers should confirm whether
diagnostic scanning is included in their approved quote before relying on it.
The physical inspection and the scan, when available, should inform each other;
neither replaces the other.

### What an inspection cannot guarantee

A visual, non-invasive PPI generally cannot guarantee the condition of hidden
internal components, detect every intermittent fault, or forecast every future
failure. Components are not disassembled unless the confirmed scope expressly
says otherwise. Location and seller rules may prevent a full lift view, road
test, extended operation, removal of covers, or access to records and vehicle
areas.

A PPI also does not by itself establish legal title, ownership, lien status,
insurance, financing terms, emissions compliance, or every fact in the vehicle's
past. Those questions require the appropriate documents, databases, agencies,
or professionals. A history report can add context, but its records do not prove
the vehicle's complete history or present condition.

Most importantly, a PPI is not a warranty or guarantee. It is a professional
opinion about observable condition at the time and place of inspection. The
buyer retains the purchase decision.

### How to use limitations rather than ignore them

Read every limitation as a next-step prompt:

- No road test? Decide whether the seller can authorize one before purchase.
- Limited underbody access? Ask whether a suitable facility or specialist review
  is practical and worth arranging.
- A warning or code without a confirmed cause? Seek diagnosis and a current
  estimate before committing.
- An intermittent complaint that did not occur? Ask for records and consider a
  longer or specialist evaluation.
- A modified, exotic, collector, salvage, rebuilt, imported, or non-running
  vehicle? Expect individual scope review and possible specialist escalation.

An honest limit does not make the report useless. It tells you exactly where
uncertainty remains.

### CTA

[View the demonstration sample report](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/sample-report/)
to see how findings and limitations are separated. If you are considering a
Las Vegas-area vehicle, [submit a free request](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/#request)
so AutoClarity can review the exact vehicle, location, access, and scope before
quoting.

### Claims and evidence notes

- The published AutoClarity scope does not currently promise universal scan-tool
  testing. Keep the conditional scanner section unless the live facts and
  approved service scope are formally changed.
- Obtain owner confirmation of the actual tool, vehicle coverage, procedure, and
  report fields before adding any specific scan-module, readiness-monitor,
  freeze-frame, live-data, or code-history promise.
- Add original, permission-safe examples that distinguish an observation from a
  diagnosis. Label a staged or fictional example “demonstration.”
- Do not claim a scan proves codes were never cleared, a clean scan proves the
  vehicle is healthy, or any inspection finds every defect.

## Draft 4 — Sample-report explainer

- **Working title:** How to Read a Pre-Purchase Inspection Report Before You Buy
- **Primary intent:** A buyer is evaluating a PPI provider or has a report and
  wants to understand scores, priorities, photos, cost ranges, and limitations.
- **Suggested URL:** `/guides/how-to-read-a-pre-purchase-inspection-report/`
- **H1:** How to Read a Pre-Purchase Inspection Report
- **Meta description:** Learn how to read a PPI report's recommendation, safety
  concerns, findings, measurements, photos, estimated costs, and inspection
  limitations.
- **30-script connections:** Days 17, 26, 28, and 29. The existing Day 17 sample
  walkthrough is the primary video concept; do not add posting volume.

### Page body

A useful inspection report should help you answer three questions:

1. Is there anything that needs immediate attention?
2. Which findings affect the price, timing, or need for another evaluation?
3. What could not be checked, and how much uncertainty remains?

AutoClarity's public sample shows the intended structure. It is a demonstration
using a fictional VIN, mileage, and vehicle scenario—not a customer report or
evidence of a completed customer inspection. Read it to understand the format,
not as a testimonial or proof that another vehicle will have similar findings.

### 1. Start with the recommendation, then verify why it was given

The sample begins with an overall condition score and one of three recommendation
categories:

- **Proceed** — the observed condition supports moving forward within the
  report's findings and limits.
- **Negotiate / Repair First** — identified items should affect the offer,
  timing, repair plan, or need for further evaluation.
- **Do Not Proceed** — the observed concerns or unresolved risk support stopping
  the purchase in its current form.

These labels are a decision framework, not a guarantee or command. Do not accept
the headline without reading the evidence below it. Two vehicles with the same
score can have very different risks, access limits, and repair priorities.

### 2. Read immediate safety concerns before everything else

This section should identify concerns that deserve prompt attention based on
what was observed. If it says none were observed, that is not a lifetime safety
certification. It means none were observed within the inspection conditions and
scope. Read the limitations before treating any blank or “none observed” field
as complete certainty.

### 3. Separate priority, moderate, maintenance, and cosmetic findings

The report groups findings so the buyer does not have to treat every imperfection
as equally urgent. A worn safety-related item, an active operational concern, a
maintenance observation, and cosmetic wear can lead to different decisions.

For each finding, look for:

- the observable evidence;
- where on the vehicle it was found;
- a measurement or photograph when appropriate;
- how urgent the item appears within the inspection's limits;
- whether a specialist diagnosis is recommended; and
- whether any cost shown is an estimate or range rather than a repair quote.

The current sample's tire, seepage, cosmetic, brake-fluid, and filter items are
fictional demonstration content. They illustrate organization only. They must
not be presented as a real outcome, customer case, or typical savings example.

### 4. Use measurements and photos as context

Tire tread, approximate tire age, visible brake-pad information, instrument
readings, and other measurements can make an observation easier to evaluate.
They still need context: access, measurement method, manufacturer information,
vehicle use, and related condition all matter.

Photographs should show meaningful observations, not decorate the report. The
current public sample contains labeled photo placeholders. They explain where
photographic findings belong, but they are not genuine job proof. The production
content should continue to call the sample a demonstration until permission-safe
original images are available and approved.

### 5. Read the road-test and underbody sections for access

A road test may reveal behavior that cannot be evaluated while parked, but it
depends on seller permission and safe, lawful conditions. Underbody visibility
depends on the location, ground, clearance, equipment, and permission. A mobile
view is not automatically a full lift inspection.

The report should say whether those activities occurred and identify material
limits. If an important check did not occur, decide whether to arrange another
location, a specialist, or additional access before purchase.

### 6. Treat repair-cost ranges as planning inputs

Where appropriate, an estimated cost range can help the buyer budget or frame a
question for the seller. It is not a binding repair quote. Parts choice, labor,
location, diagnosis, related damage, taxes, and changing conditions can affect
the actual price. Get a current repair estimate from an appropriate provider
when the amount will influence the purchase.

AutoClarity does not negotiate with the seller for the buyer and does not sell
the repairs it identifies. The buyer decides how to use the documented findings.

### 7. Finish with the limitations

The limitations section is part of the result, not boilerplate to skip. A PPI is
a professional opinion about observable condition at a point in time. It is not
a warranty and cannot reveal every hidden, intermittent, or future defect.
Components are not disassembled unless expressly included, and seller or
location access may constrain the work.

After reading the limits, write down any unresolved question that could change
your decision. That list tells you whether you are ready to proceed, should seek
another evaluation, need to renegotiate, or should stop.

### CTA

[Open the AutoClarity demonstration report](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/sample-report/)
and compare its findings, measurements, and limitations with the decision you
are facing. For a Las Vegas-area vehicle, [request an inspection for
review](/las-vegas-pre-purchase-inspection/#request). Submitting is free and is
not an appointment or charge.

### Claims and evidence notes

- Keep “demonstration,” “fictional,” and “not a customer report” visible anywhere
  the sample scenario or its findings appear.
- The current sample has labeled photo placeholders. Do not call them real
  photographs or photographic proof. Replace or supplement them only with
  owner-approved, permission-safe evidence and a documented privacy review.
- Recheck report fields and recommendation labels against the live sample before
  publication; do not promise a field that the actual customer report does not
  consistently provide.
- Do not imply that a condition score is standardized across the industry, that
  estimated costs are quotes, or that a recommendation guarantees an outcome.

### Dealership or private-seller location

A road test requires seller permission and safe, lawful conditions. Underbody
review depends on the location, ground, vehicle clearance, permission, safety,
and available equipment. A full lift inspection may require a suitable facility
arranged in advance and separately reflected in the approved scope or quote.

If access is limited, the written result should identify the material limitation.
That gives the buyer a chance to seek another location or additional specialist
review rather than assuming an inaccessible area was checked.
